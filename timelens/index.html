c. 1795 · The contextNeptune vs. Vulcan

The great geology war

When the encyclopedia reached BASALT, it walked into a fight already decades old: was this black rock laid down by water, or born of fire?

Neptunists · water

Abraham Gottlob Werner

From Freiberg, Werner taught that every rock — basalt included — had precipitated out of a universal ocean.

Vulcanists · fire

James Hutton

Hutton argued basalt's veins and columns were injected molten into older strata — born of the Earth's own heat.

1810 · The recordBritannica, 4th ed. — “Mineralogy”

An encyclopedia hedges

“No subject in geological speculation has produced more controversy than the origin of basalt; one party asserting that it is the effect of fire, another that it has been deposited from an aqueous solution. Our limits preclude us even from literally stating the arguments…”

What the page could report was experiment:

Before the blowpipe, melts into an opaque black glass Affects the magnetic needle — “the great proportion of iron” A ringing sound under the hammer Regular columns at Staffa, the Hebrides & the Giant's Causeway

Quotation lightly cleaned from machine OCR for legibility; the original scan and run artifacts are preserved unchanged. The page itself declines to pick a side — the hedge is part of the record.

1830s · The reversalBritannica, 7th ed. — “Mineralogy / Geology”

The same book changes its mind

A few editions later the hedge is gone. The seventh edition files the basalts of Staffa and Ireland “later than the chalk” — and says what the fourth could not:

Basalts “identical with products of active volcanoes” — the action “of the same kind as that exerted by an active volcano.” It was, in fact, volcanic.

The controversy was not settled by an argument. It was settled in print, edition by edition, by the same encyclopedia that once refused to pick a side.

Quotation lightly cleaned from machine OCR of the scanned seventh-edition page; the original scan is preserved alongside the fourth-edition one. This is the lens's favorite kind of evidence: Britannica versus Britannica.

Today · The resolutionWikipedia — “Basalt”, “Igneous textures”

Settled: fire won.

“Basalt is a common igneous rock.”

Lava that crystallized too fast for its grains to be seen — an aphanitic texture, in the modern vocabulary. The columns the fourth edition could only describe are now understood as cooling joints, and the rock the Neptunists owed to the ocean floors the ocean itself.

Cooled lava — the Vulcanists were right Aphanitic — crystals too fine to see unaided Columnar jointing — Giant's Causeway The ocean floor is mostly basalt
