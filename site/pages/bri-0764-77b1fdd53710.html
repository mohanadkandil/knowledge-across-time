The tides are periodical oscillations in the watersTides of the ocean, which take place twice every twenty-four hours, and are produced by the attraction of the moon and the sun. In the open sea, they are at their height three hours after the moon has passed the meridian of the place, and the meridian opposite. They are smallest towards the pasly, and greatest at the equator, where, however, they do not exceed two or three feet in the great ocean. Their greatest elevation times place in narrow seas, where the section of the moon is aided by winds, currents, the position of the coast, etc. The highest tides known are in the Gulf of St Malis, where the flood, driven back by the coast of England, rises to the height of seven of eight chains. Inland seas, whose entrances face the west, have rarely my life, because the moon acts on all their parts at once, and there are no lateral waters to flow in and produce a local elevation. In the Greenland Sea the temperature increases with the depth; at a depth of 2750 fathoms the temperature was found to be 8°, and bottom waters at Spitsbergen are from 10° to 20° above the mean temperature of the climate.

language

Historical source claim

The most plausible explanation of this anomaly is, that the westerly current from Now Zenbia, residual specifically light in the field water of the great rivers of Northern Asia, may rise above the branch of the Gulf Stream which penetrates to these latitude, and the warm waters of the latter will thus be found on the bottom.

Modern source claim

Along the eastern part flows the warm Spitsbergen Current, a part of Gulf Stream. Mixtures of cold, freshwater ice melt and the warm, salty Spitsbergen Current may experience cabbeling, which might contribute to thermohaline circulation.

Both texts discuss warm Atlantic/Gulf Stream water entering the region, but the historical text offers a conjectural explanation for warm bottom water involving river water from Northern Asia, while the modern text describes the Spitsbergen Current and cabbeling as part of established oceanographic understanding.
