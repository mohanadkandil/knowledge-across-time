Similarities

A commodate/commodatum is a gratuitous loan for use of a movable or immovable thing for a certain time, with the same individual thing to be returned.

A commodate does not transfer property, and the thing must be returned in essence and without deterioration.

Commodian was a Christian Latin poet.

Differences: language, knowledge and coverage

knowledge

Historical source claim

Britannica states Commodianus wrote in the 4th century.

Modern source claim

Wikipedia states Commodian flourished about AD 250 (3rd century).

The two sources assign Commodian to different centuries, representing a difference in historical dating.

coverage

Historical source claim

Britannica's entry for COMMODORE describes a naval officer in the British marines with the rank of brigadier-general and a broad pennant.

Modern source claim

Wikipedia's Commodore page is primarily a disambiguation list covering many meanings, including naval ranks, automobiles, computers, and other uses.

The historical source provides a specific definition of the naval rank, while the modern source offers broad coverage across many topics rather than a single focused article.

language

Historical source claim

Britannica uses archaic spellings such as "ju-tifrondence," "concellion," "fans individual faces," and "grains."

Modern source claim

Wikipedia uses modern standardized English such as "civil law," "concession," "same individual," and "gratis."

The historical text exhibits OCR noise and eighteenth-century orthography, whereas the modern text uses contemporary spelling and terminology.

Uncertainties

  • The historical Britannica text is heavily degraded by OCR errors, making some phrases difficult to interpret precisely (e.g., "cane officers of referring again the fans individual faces").
  • The historical Britannica entry for COMMODORE is garbled, so the precise historical description of the rank cannot be reliably reconstructed from the supplied text alone.
  • The Wikipedia Commodian article gives a different century (about AD 250) than Britannica (4th century), and the supplied texts do not explain the discrepancy.
