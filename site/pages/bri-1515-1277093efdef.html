Two strong matches were found: the historical Britannica entry on EGISTHUS corresponds to the Wikipedia article on Aegisthus, and the historical entry on EGOSPOTAMOS corresponds to the Wikipedia article on Aegospotami. Both pairs agree on the core facts—Aegisthus's parentage by Thyestes and Pelopia, his seven-year rule of Mycenae, and his death by Orestes; and Aegospotami's location in the Thracian Chersonese at the Hellespont, the Athenian defeat by Lysander, and the end of the Peloponnesian War. Major differences include the severe OCR corruption of the historical text, the vastly greater narrative detail in the modern Wikipedia articles, and the addition of specific dates and modern geographical identifications in the Wikipedia coverage. Differences: language, knowledge and coverage

language

Historical source claim

The Britannica text uses archaic OCR-corrupted spellings such as "bifory," "fun," "Pilipicia," "Myceems," and "Cytecenneira."

Modern source claim

The Wikipedia text uses modern standardized spellings: "history," "son," "Pelopia," "Mycenae," and "Clytemnestra."

The historical passage is heavily degraded by OCR errors, while the modern passage is in clean contemporary English.

coverage

Historical source claim

The Britannica entry for EGISTHUS is brief and does not mention Aegisthus's seduction of Clytemnestra, the murder of Agamemnon, or the details of Thyestes' revenge against Atreus.

Modern source claim

The Wikipedia article provides extensive narrative detail, including Aegisthus's affair with Clytemnestra, their joint murder of Agamemnon, the sacrifice of Iphigenia, and Orestes' revenge.

The modern article covers a much broader mythological narrative than the concise historical encyclopedia entry.

coverage

Historical source claim

The Britannica entry for EGOSPOTAMOS mentions the battle and the end of the war but does not give a date for the battle or discuss the river's identification with modern streams.

Modern source claim

The Wikipedia article dates the battle to 405 BC, identifies the river with modern Karakova Dere and Büyük Dere/Münipbey Deresi, and notes a meteorite fall in 467 BC.

The modern article adds chronological precision, geographical identification, and ancillary historical anecdotes absent from the older source.

uncertain

Historical source claim

The Britannica text states that Aegisthus was "tackled by a gent" and "called _Egifkar_," and that he was exposed in the woods.

Modern source claim

The Wikipedia text states that Aegisthus was abandoned by his mother, raised by shepherds, and suckled by a goat, hence his name from αἴξ (male goat).

The OCR-corrupted historical text is too garbled to determine whether it agrees or disagrees with the modern account of Aegisthus's exposure and naming.
