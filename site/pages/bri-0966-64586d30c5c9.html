Summary

The historical Britannica seventh edition text discusses the Tertiary period as an active geological subject, focusing on European strata, volcanic activity, disturbances, and elevations, while expressing uncertainty about precise dating and boundaries. The candidate Wikipedia passages describe the Tertiary as an obsolete geologic period spanning roughly 66 to 2.6 million years ago, now replaced by the Paleogene and Neogene periods, with formal subdivisions and standardized dating. Both sources agree that the Tertiary's boundaries are difficult to define and that it relates to relatively recent geological deposits, but they differ in that the historical source treats it as a current field of study with regional European examples, whereas the modern source classifies it as a superseded term with precise, internationally defined subdivisions.

Similarities

The Tertiary is a geologic period/assemblage of deposits with uncertain or debated boundaries and dating.

The term Tertiary is no longer formally recognized by the International Commission on Stratigraphy and has been replaced by Paleogene and Neogene.

Charles Lyell developed a detailed classification of the Tertiary based on fossil mollusks and subdivided it into epochs.

Differences: language, knowledge and coverage

knowledge

Historical source claim

The Britannica text treats the Tertiary as a current, active geological concept with ongoing research into volcanic activity, strata disturbances, and elevation events in Europe.

Modern source claim

The Wikipedia text states the Tertiary is an obsolete geologic period no longer recognized by the ICS, with its timespan split into Paleogene and Neogene.

The historical source presents Tertiary geology as a field of live investigation with specific European examples (e.g., Etna, Auvergne, the Rhine, the Alps), while the modern source frames it as a historical term that has been formally superseded.

language

Historical source claim

The Britannica text uses archaic and OCR-degraded language (e.g., "tertiary em," "tertiary producers," "tertiary books," "Damball," "Rhin," "Pyrament chain").

Modern source claim

The Wikipedia text uses standardized modern geological terminology (e.g., "Cenozoic Era," "Paleogene," "Neogene," "Pliocene," "Gelasian Stage").

The language differs due to the historical period of the Britannica seventh edition and OCR noise, whereas the Wikipedia text reflects current stratigraphic nomenclature.

coverage

Historical source claim

The Britannica text focuses extensively on European geological features, including the Rhine, Hungary, northern Germany, Poland, Russia, the Alps, the Pyrenees, Sicily, and the British Isles.

Modern source claim

The Wikipedia text provides a global, formal definition of the Tertiary period and its subdivisions without the same regional European emphasis.

The historical source offers detailed regional geological observations, while the modern source gives a broad, standardized chronostratigraphic overview.

uncertain

Historical source claim

The Britannica text expresses uncertainty about the precise geography, date, and causes of Tertiary elevations, calling it "one of the most difficult of geological problems."

Modern source claim

The Wikipedia text provides specific numerical dates for the Tertiary (66 million to 2.6 or 1.8 million years ago) and its subdivisions, presenting them as established facts.

The historical source emphasizes uncertainty and difficulty in dating Tertiary events, while the modern source supplies precise numerical boundaries, though it notes the end date has been redefined.
