Summary

The historical Britannica seventh-edition text on London and the modern Wikipedia article on the London Wall both describe a Roman defensive wall around London that fell into disrepair after Roman rule and was restored under Alfred the Great. Both mention a length of about two miles and gates such as Bishopsgate, Aldgate, and Cripplegate. However, the Britannica text is heavily OCR-corrupted, introducing numerous unreadable names and dates, and it reflects 19th-century antiquarian uncertainty about the wall's construction (attributing it possibly to Constantine or Theodosius) and its episcopal status. The Wikipedia article provides a clear archaeological date of c. AD 200, precise dimensions, and details about the riverside wall, medieval gates, and modern conservation. Because of the OCR corruption, many specific claims in the Britannica text cannot be reliably compared, and some passages have no intelligible modern counterpart.

Differences: language, knowledge and coverage

language

Historical source claim

The Britannica text is heavily corrupted by OCR errors, rendering names and dates unreliable (e.g., "Egbert, 9119", "King Elizabeth and this son Abethelol", "Dunes", "Sexzon").

Modern source claim

The Wikipedia text uses standard modern English and provides clear dates and names (e.g., Alfred the Great, c. AD 200, c. 410).

The historical source's text quality prevents reliable extraction of specific claims, while the modern source is legible and precise.

knowledge

Historical source claim

Britannica attributes the construction of the walls to Constantine or Theodosius, with uncertainty about the date, and states London was erected into an episcopal city by that emperor after conversion to Christianity.

Modern source claim

Wikipedia dates the wall to c. AD 200, built around Londinium, and does not attribute its construction to Constantine or Theodosius or link it to an episcopal foundation.

The historical source reflects 19th-century antiquarian speculation about Roman emperors and Christian bishops, whereas the modern source relies on archaeological dating to c. AD 200.

coverage

Historical source claim

Britannica covers London's history from Roman times through the Danish invasions and Alfred's restoration, including details of the wall's course, gates, height (twenty-two feet), and towers (fifteen).

Modern source claim

Wikipedia covers the wall's Roman construction, post-Roman disrepair, Anglo-Saxon restoration, medieval development, gates, dimensions (8 to 10 ft wide, up to 20 ft high), riverside wall, and modern survival/conservation.

The sources cover overlapping historical periods but differ in depth and focus: Britannica emphasizes the historical narrative of London's recovery, while Wikipedia emphasizes the physical wall, its archaeology, and surviving remains.

uncertain

Historical source claim

Britannica expresses doubt about the date of the wall's creation, citing competing authorities (Wookwerp/Constantin vs. Maandl/Theodosius, year 369) and the reliability of the Antonine itineraries.

Modern source claim

Wikipedia states the wall was built around c. AD 200 and does not express the same antiquarian uncertainty about Constantine or Theodosius.

The historical source treats the wall's dating as uncertain and dependent on ancient authors, while the modern source presents a consolidated archaeological date.

  • The historical source's attribution of the wall to Constantine or Theodosius cannot be reconciled with the modern c. AD 200 dating without external knowledge, which is not permitted.
  • The extent to which the Britannica description of the wall's course and gates matches the medieval versus Roman wall is uncertain due to OCR corruption.
