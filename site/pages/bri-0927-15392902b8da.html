Summary

The Britannica excerpt concerns the historical pharmacy preparation known as water of kali, including recipes, purity testing, and medical use of alkaline lixivia. The only topically relevant Wikipedia passage, on Salsola kali, confirms that kali plants were burned and leached with water to obtain alkali, but it frames the product as soda ash for glass and cleaning rather than as a medicine. The sources overlap on the basic idea of extracting alkali from kali with water, but differ in purpose, terminology, and level of pharmaceutical detail.

Modern source claim

The Wikipedia article describes the general historical production of soda ash from Salsola kali ashes but gives no pharmaceutical recipe, dosage, or medical application.

The historical source treats water of kali as a pharmacy preparation with detailed technical directions, while the modern source covers the plant's industrial and etymological history without matching the Britannica procedural detail.

knowledge

Historical source claim

Britannica presents water of kali as a medicinal alkali used in pharmacy, with discussion of its purity, strength, and neutrality of oils and fats.

Modern source claim

Wikipedia describes soda ash from Salsola kali as used primarily to make glass and secondarily as a cleaning agent, not as a medicine.

The historical frame is pharmaceutical and therapeutic; the modern frame is industrial and botanical.

language

Historical source claim

Britannica uses early modern pharmacy terminology such as "lixivium," "phamaspects," "kali," and "fixed alkaline fats."

Modern source claim

Wikipedia uses modern chemical terms such as sodium carbonate, potassium carbonate, and soda ash, and notes the Arabic etymology al-qali.

The vocabulary differs substantially due to changes in chemistry and pharmacy nomenclature.
