Summary

The historical Britannica pages describe fish genera SILURUS, PLATSYACUS, LORICATA, and ANABAS with morphological details and species lists. Modern Wikipedia counterparts exist for Silurus, Platystacus, Loricaria, and Anabas. Strong matches are found for Silurus (including S. glanis), Platystacus, and Loricaria, though geographic and species-list coverage differs. Anabas is a partial match: both sources use the name, but the historical text associates it with South America and unusual eye anatomy, while the modern source describes Asian climbing gouramies with a labyrinth organ. OCR noise and archaic language in the historical text introduce uncertainty in exact comparisons.

Counterparts and relevance

strong

Britannica's Gen. 4. SILURUS describes catfishes with barbels, thick lips, dentated jaws, and elongated compressed bodies, and lists species such as glanis (European silurus), electricus (electric catfish), and others. The modern Wikipedia article on Silurus confirms it is a genus of catfishes native to Europe and Asia and lists Silurus glanis (Wels catfish) among 20 recognized species.

strong

Britannica's Gen. 5. PLATSYACUS describes a fish with a mouth beneath, bearded with cirri, depressed body, and long compressed tail, and mentions Platystacus species native to Indian seas/rivers. The modern Wikipedia article on Platystacus identifies it as a genus of banjo catfish in the family Aspredinidae, monotypic with Platystacus cotylephorus, found in coastal waters and lower river portions of northern South America.

strong

Britannica's Gen. 6. LORICATA describes fishes with smooth depressed heads, toothless mouths, six-rayed gill membranes, and bodies covered with hard crust/scutes, listing species such as Ribbed loricaria and Soldier loricaria native to Indian and American seas. The modern Wikipedia article on Loricaria identifies it as a genus of freshwater ray-finned fishes in the family Loricariidae (suckermouth armored catfishes), found in South America.

partial

Britannica includes a genus Anabas with a description of a fish having a depressed head, terminal teeth, protuberant eyes with double pupils, and five-rayed gill mountaine, plus a species Ibulmus from South America (Surinam). The modern Wikipedia article on Anabas describes climbing gouramies native to southern and eastern Asia with a labyrinth organ for breathing atmospheric oxygen, which is not present in the Britannica text.

Similarities

Silurus is a genus of catfishes native to Europe and Asia, and Silurus glanis is a recognized species.

Platystacus is a genus of catfish with a depressed body and long compressed tail.

Loricaria is a genus of armored catfishes with bodies covered in hard plates/scutes, found in South America.

Differences: language, knowledge and coverage

knowledge

Historical source claim

Britannica describes Anabas species including Ibulmus from South America, specifically Surinam, with double pupils that upon dissection apply only to one half of the organ.

Modern source claim

Wikipedia describes Anabas as climbing gouramies native to southern and eastern Asia, possessing a labyrinth organ for breathing atmospheric oxygen, with species found in India, Sri Lanka, Bangladesh, Burma, Indonesia, Malaysia, Thailand, Cambodia, and the Philippines.

The historical text places Anabas/Ibulmus in South America (Surinam) and describes ocular anatomy, while the modern source places Anabas in Asia and describes a labyrinth organ; these are different biological understandings and geographic ranges.

coverage

Historical source claim

Britannica lists many additional Silurus species facies such as militaris, larges, bericharge, terarum, gelatin, moldyta, himmatinas, flyt cinna, clarias, grandecimanilang, erythogerea, borrachus, fufilris, vulatus, abbirindes, aficus, myusia, angulilaris, undatomasia, various, felis, cours, carrinus, stones, ciliatings, and blood.

Modern source claim

Wikipedia lists 20 recognized Silurus species, including aristotelis, asotus, biwaensis, burmanensis, caobangensis, chantrei, dakrongensis, duanensis, glanis, grahami, langsonensis, lanzhouensis, lithophilus, longibarbatus, mento, meridionalis, microdorsalis, soldatovi, tomodai, triostegus, plus two fossil species.

The historical source uses older or variant species names and lists many more facies/species than the modern recognized list; coverage differs substantially.

coverage

Historical source claim

Britannica's LORICATA includes species described as native to Indian and American seas, including a 'Soldier laricaria' native of South Amer and a 'Spekkel laricaria' native of the river of Siberian.

Modern source claim

Wikipedia states Loricaria species are found in South America, distributed east of the Andes in tropical and subtropical parts, with species in various river habitats.

The historical text attributes some loricaria species to Indian seas and Siberia, whereas the modern source restricts the genus to South America east of the Andes.

language

Historical source claim

Britannica uses archaic/OCR-affected spellings such as 'filursas' for silurus, 'dorfal' for dorsal, 'depreffed' for depressed, 'compreffed' for compressed, and 'fees' for seas.

Modern source claim

Wikipedia uses modern standardized taxonomic and anatomical terminology.

The difference is primarily orthographic and reflects historical printing/OCR rather than a factual disagreement.

Uncertainties

  • The Britannica text is heavily affected by OCR errors and archaic typography (long s rendered as f, ligatures, misspellings), making precise taxonomic attribution difficult for several entries.
  • The historical Britannica entry for Anabas places Ibulmus in South America/Surinam, while the modern Wikipedia article places Anabas in Asia; it is unclear whether the historical 'Anabas' refers to the same modern genus or a different historical grouping.
  • The historical text lists numerous Silurus 'facies' with names that do not clearly map to modern recognized species, so it is uncertain which, if any, are synonyms versus obsolete or misapplied names.
  • The historical LORICATA entry mentions species native to Indian seas and Siberia, which conflicts with the modern South American distribution; it is unclear whether these are misidentifications, different historical genera, or OCR/geographic errors.
