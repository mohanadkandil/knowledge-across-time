Summary

The historical Britannica entry and the Wikipedia articles on Arthur Johnston (poet) concern the same individual, a Scottish Neo-Latin poet and physician of the early seventeenth century. Both agree on his Scottish origins, education and time abroad, his 1637 Aberdeen publication of a poetic Psalms paraphrase, and his 1637 Amsterdam anthology of Scottish Latin verse. They differ on details of his personal life (two wives, long French residence in Britannica only), his royal connections (physician to the king in Britannica versus rector and Laud's patronage in Wikipedia), and the precise circumstances of his death. The historical text is substantially degraded by OCR errors, making some of its claims difficult to verify against the modern account.

Similarities

Arthur Johnston was a Scottish poet and physician who lived in the early seventeenth century.

Johnston studied at or was associated with the University of Aberdeen and spent time abroad, including in France and Italy.

Johnston published a poetic paraphrase of the Psalms in 1637 at Aberdeen.

Johnston published an anthology of contemporary Latin verse by Scottish poets in 1637 at Amsterdam.

Johnston died in 1641.

Differences: language, knowledge and coverage

knowledge

Historical source claim

Britannica states Johnston spent twenty years in France and had two wives of different nations, one married in France and the other from the vicinity of Mehlin in Brabant.

Modern source claim

Wikipedia states Johnston lived at Sedan as professor at the Academy of Sedan, was in practice in Paris in 1619, and returned to Scotland about 1625; it does not mention two wives or a wife from Mehlin.

The historical entry includes biographical details about marriages and a long residence in France that are absent from the Wikipedia article; this may reflect different source bases or OCR corruption in the historical text.

knowledge

Historical source claim

Britannica says Benson conjectures Johnston was appointed physician to the king in 1633, but notes the conjecture is refined by little-type in a publication.

Modern source claim

Wikipedia states Johnston met William Laud at Charles I's Scottish coronation in 1633, was encouraged by Laud in his literary efforts, and was appointed rector of King's College, Aberdeen, in June 1637.

The two sources attribute different roles and patrons to Johnston in the 1630s; the historical text suggests a royal medical appointment, while Wikipedia emphasizes a literary connection with Laud and an academic rectorship.

language

Historical source claim

The historical text uses archaic and OCR-corrupted language, such as "university of Seattle" and "Paraphmasis poetic Pal-morma Javidla."

Modern source claim

Wikipedia uses standardized modern English and Latin titles, e.g., "University of Aberdeen" and "Psalmorum Davidis paraphrasis poetica et canticorum evangelicorum."

Differences are largely due to historical spelling, OCR errors, and modern editorial normalization rather than substantive factual disagreement.

coverage

Historical source claim

Britannica discusses Johnston's poetic controversy with Dr. Egblicam over Buchanan's psalm version and his poem 'Comedian College Medical Parafensis de Mania G. Eglishemii.'

Modern source claim

Wikipedia does not mention the controversy with Egblicam or this specific poem, but does mention the later pamphlet controversy begun by Ruddiman's Vindication of Buchanan's paraphrase.

The historical entry covers an earlier literary dispute in detail, while Wikipedia focuses on Johnston's major works and a later eighteenth-century controversy.

Uncertainties

  • The Britannica text is heavily OCR-corrupted (e.g., "university of Seattle," "counnyman Andrew Melvic," "Paraphmasis poetic Pal-morma Javidla"), so some historical claims may be misreadings rather than the original text.
  • The historical Britannica entry mentions two wives and a twenty-year stay in France, while Wikipedia does not; it is unclear whether this reflects lost information, OCR error, or differing biographical traditions.
  • The exact year and place of Johnston's death are stated differently or ambiguously across the sources: Britannica's text is garbled around death dates, while Wikipedia states he died at Oxford in 1641.
  • Whether Johnston was appointed physician to the king (Britannica, via Benson's conjecture) or rector of King's College (Wikipedia) remains unresolved from the supplied texts alone.
