Summary

The historical Britannica supplement describes China's legal system in divisional terms, including sacred rites, palace regulations, military governance under the "ping-pos," frontier protection, and a criminal code administered by the "Hing-pos," with severe punishments including death for homicide, robbery, and other offenses, as well as provisions allowing husbands to kill unfaithful wives and parents to sell children, with uncertainty about the extent of infanticide. The modern Wikipedia corpus on the Great Qing Legal Code describes a code of 436 articles in seven parts aligned with the Six Ministries, based on the Ming Code, with 1,907 statutes across revisions from 1644 to 1912, many offenses punishable by death, and lasting influence on subsequent Chinese legal systems. Both sources agree on a divisional ministry-based structure and a detailed penal code with capital punishments, but differ in language clarity (due to OCR corruption in the historical text), specific statutory coverage, and the level of quantitative detail provided.

Modern source claim
