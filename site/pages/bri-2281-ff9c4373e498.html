Summary

The historical Britannica supplement discusses the trisection of an angle, describing a conchoid curve construction and noting three possible positions for the trisecting line, with a mention of Newton's contribution. The modern Wikipedia corpus confirms that angle trisection is impossible with straightedge and compass alone (proved by Wantzel in 1837) but solvable with additional tools such as neusis construction, which was used by Archimedes, Pappus, and Newton. Both sources agree that the problem requires going beyond elementary straightedge-and-compass geometry, but they differ in terminology, mathematical rigor, and the specific constructions described.

Differences: language, knowledge and coverage

language

Historical source claim

The problem is called "triscion" or "trisection" and the solution curve is called the "Conchoid," with branches named "interior conchoid" and "extterior conchoid."

Modern source claim

The problem is called "angle trisection," and the tool-based method is called "neusis construction" or "marked straightedge."

The historical text uses older terminology ("triscion," "Conchoid") while the modern corpus uses standardized terms ("angle trisection," "neusis construction").

knowledge

Historical source claim

The trisection problem admits three distinct positions of the trisecting line and is solved by a conchoid curve; the text does not state a formal impossibility proof for straightedge and compass.

Modern source claim

Pierre Wantzel proved in 1837 that angle trisection is impossible for arbitrary angles using only straightedge and compass, via field extensions and Galois theory.

The historical supplement treats the problem as a construction challenge solvable with higher curves, whereas the modern article establishes a rigorous impossibility result for the classical tools.

coverage

Historical source claim

The Britannica supplement provides detailed geometric constructions with labeled points (A, B, C, D, E, F, G) and arc relationships, including assertions about arcs being two-thirds of the circumference.

Modern source claim

The Wikipedia article covers algebraic criteria (triple-angle formula, cubic polynomials, field extensions), approximation methods, origami, linkages, and specific constructible angles, but does not reproduce the historical conchoid construction in detail.

The historical source focuses on a specific curve-based geometric construction, while the modern source emphasizes algebraic impossibility and alternative modern methods.

uncertain

Historical source claim

The text attributes the conchoid to "Mercedes" (likely an OCR or historical error for Nicomedes) and describes a geometric construction with OCR-corrupted notation.

Modern source claim

No corresponding claim about "Mercedes" appears in the Wikipedia corpus.

The historical text's attribution and some notation are unclear due to OCR artifacts, making precise comparison difficult.

Uncertainties

  • The historical text contains OCR errors (e.g., "triscion," "infecting," "Mercedes," "angent") that obscure precise claims and attributions.
  • It is unclear whether the historical "Conchoid" is identical to the neusis construction described in the modern sources, or a distinct curve-based method.
  • The Britannica supplement does not explicitly state that trisection is impossible with straightedge and compass; it only says elementary geometry will not furnish the means, leaving the status of the problem ambiguous by modern standards.
