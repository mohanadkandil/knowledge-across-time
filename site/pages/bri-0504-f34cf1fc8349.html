Summary

The historical Britannica passage on dyeing discusses the use of substances such as alum, tartar, vitriolated tartar, and green vitriol to fix coloring matter to cloth, describing a theory of adhesion and separation of color particles. The modern Wikipedia article on mordants defines a mordant as a dye fixative that forms a coordination complex with the dye to bind it to fabric, lists alum and metal salts as mordants, and notes that mordant type affects color shade and fastness. Both sources agree that mordant-like substances are used to make dye adhere to fabric and that different substances produce different colors. However, the historical explanation relies on adhesion to gummy and resinous matter and specific earths, while the modern explanation uses coordination chemistry and dye lakes. The historical text also treats indigo as an exception to the need for mordants, a point not elaborated in the modern corpus. OCR corruption in the historical text and the absence of Hellot's theory or detailed indigo chemistry in the modern corpus limit the precision of the comparison.

Similarities

Mordants are substances used to fix or set dyes on fabrics so the color holds fast.

Alum is used as a mordant in dyeing.

The type of mordant used affects the shade or color of the dyed fabric.

Indigo is an exception to the general rule that mordants are needed to give body and permanency to dye color.

Differences: language, knowledge and coverage

knowledge

Historical source claim

The Britannica text proposes that coloring particles are insoluble in water, spirit of wine, and alkaline liquors, and that their diffusion in these liquids is useful merely for their adhesion to gummy and resinous particles, from which they can be separated by applying a material to which they have greater adhesive power, such as alum, green vitriol, or other earths.

Modern source claim

A mordant forms a coordination complex with the dye, which then attaches to the fabric; the resulting dye lake is an insoluble complex that increases fastness because the higher molecular weight dye is bonded to the fibre.

The historical text describes adhesion and separation of coloring particles via gummy and resinous matter and specific earths, while the modern article explains the mechanism in terms of coordination complexes and dye lakes, reflecting a different chemical understanding.

language

Historical source claim

The historical text uses archaic and OCR-corrupted language such as "fubliances," "likivions," "infangaged," "refonos," and "thofe."

Modern source claim

The modern article uses standard contemporary terminology such as "coordination complex," "dye fixative," "substrate," and "fastness."

The language differs substantially due to historical spelling, OCR errors, and the evolution of technical vocabulary in chemistry and textile dyeing.

coverage

Historical source claim

The Britannica text discusses Hellot's theory involving tartar and vitriolated tartar, preparatory falls, boiling water, pores of the fluff, and the role of fixed alkali, acids, and neutral salts in separating coloring particles from vegetable matters.

Modern source claim

The Wikipedia article covers pre-mordanting, meta-mordanting, and post-mordanting methods; the effect of mordants on substrate damage; stability of dye lakes; residual iron mordant causing dye rot; and the use of mordants in histology for stains such as haematoxylin and eosin.

The historical source focuses on 18th-century dyeing theory and specific substances like tartar and vitriol, while the modern source covers mordant application methods, industrial displacement by substantive dyes, and histological uses not present in the historical text.

uncertain

Historical source claim

The Britannica text states that indigo is very difficult of solution but may be distilled by alkaline fats, concentrated oil of virilol, orpiment, or combinations of half-quickokine, and that when a piece of fluff is put into the lotion it remains green while immersed but resumes its native color upon contact with air.

Modern source claim

The provided Wikipedia corpus does not contain a detailed modern account of indigo dyeing chemistry or vat dyeing beyond the disambiguation listing for indigo dye.

The historical description of indigo's behavior in alkaline solution and its reversion to blue upon air exposure is not directly addressed by the available modern passages, so a comparison cannot be reliably made.

Uncertainties

  • The Britannica text is heavily corrupted by OCR errors (e.g., "fubliances," "likivions," "infangaged," "thofe," "Hellot" vs "Heilot"), making precise historical claims difficult to verify from the supplied text alone.
  • The historical text references "Mr Hellot" and his theory of tartar and vitriolated tartar, but the modern corpus does not discuss Hellot or this specific theory, so it is unclear whether the modern view confirms, refutes, or simply supersedes it.
  • The historical text's discussion of indigo being "distilled" by alkaline fats, oil of vitriol, orpiment, and other substances is obscure and may reflect OCR errors or obsolete terminology; no comparable modern explanation is present in the corpus.
